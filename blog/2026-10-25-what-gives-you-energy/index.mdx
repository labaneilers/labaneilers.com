One of the more surprisingly important abilities I've acquired over the years: an acute awareness of my own psychic fuel gauge. After all these years, I feel like I've finally got a pretty good handle on what things feed my creativity, give me energy and momentum, and what things slowly drain me- sapping my resilience and leaving me feeling spent.

Some I've known for a long time- they're what got me into this field to begin with. For instance, I know that days I spend building stuff with my team, exploring problem spaces, doing research, running experiments, and getting new features released to customers- these are the kind of days that get me excited to get out of bed and get to work.

{/* truncate */}

Other things, it took me longer to figure out. I now know that time spent on people and organizational issues tend to drain my battery. It costs me a slow trickle of energy for routine, everyday ceremonies, one-on-ones, and administrivia. Calendar fragmentation takes a toll; especially when dominated by meetings that feel like cognitive speed bumps- tangential to what I had my heart set on accomplishing that day.

Dealing with the negative aspects of human relationships takes a lot more energy. Difficult conversations, addressing poor performance, dealing with history, mistrust, and thorny unspoken grudges- it's really important to surface and tackle these deliberately, but man, this is when I really feel my CPU fan come on.

Its a bit ironic, because I think I've become fairly decent at management, and I know it's important work. I even volunteer to help with these kinds of problems sometimes, especially when I think I'm well positioned to help. This unfortunately doesn't change what it does to my body.

## Getting dangerously close to burnout

About a decade ago, after years of operating primarily as a player-coach, I allowed myself to be drafted into a director position where I was helping run a bunch of teams, full-time. Admittedly, a lot of the work was stuff I love: product and architectural strategy, building alignment around a vision, and driving delivery. 

Maybe in an ideal organization, that would have been the bulk of way I spent my time- but it just wasn't in my case. In practice, most of my days were spent doing exactly the kinds of things that I now recognize as more depleting than replenishing.

After 3 years of this, I got dangerously close to burning out. I didn't quite understand what was happening, because I was in exactly the kind of position I had been working towards for my whole career. I was enjoying having a broad impact, and more organizational influence than I'd ever had before.

For the first time in my adult life, I stopped looking forward to going to work. I felt perpetually shitty in a way I'd never felt before, and it wasn't going away.

In a lucky coincidence, I had dinner with an old colleague around this time, who offered me a new job in a new industry. His offer put into concrete terms something I hadn't yet come to grips with, and helped me see my own situation more objectively. I was so caught up in the idea of what my job meant, but I had lost sight of how *it felt to do my job every day*.

I ended up taking the new job, which I was able to structure around the things I really wanted to work on. I started to feel better almost immediately, and I realized shortly thereafter how close I had come to running myself completely into the ground.

Since then, it's been pretty clear to me that while I do enjoy being well paid, the correlation with my level of happiness has weakened significantly. I've explicitly decided to sacrifice both for the privilege of getting to spend more of my days doing the things that keep my psychic fuel tank filled- and fewer days doing the things that drain it.

## Aaron's story

Just to be clear: I recognize being in this position is a *huge* privilege. Most humans who've ever lived on Earth haven't had the luxury of choosing what kind of work they got to do. I'm extremely lucky to have been born in a time and place where an increasing number of people don't have to spend the majority of their lives focused on *just surviving*, but have the freedom to explore what it means for them to *flourish*.

I often think about my grandfather, Aaron, who barely survived a childhood of desperate poverty in Poland, before immigrating to the US when he was 13. He spoke no English, and had only a few years of grade school education. At the time he married my grandmother, he was studying English in night classes while working days in a shoe factory. When his family was threatened by union-busting mobsters, he used the savings they had scraped together to open a gas station repair shop. After a decade, he traded up for the opportunity to do something that didn't involve spending his days covered in engine grease: driving a cab.

Aaron never got a chance to do work that got him excited to get out of bed; he had to do it to keep his family fed and housed. And like most immigrants in the US, he did it give his children choices that he never had.

He died when I was just a teenager, but not before he got to see his children's lives unfold in directions that were never available to him. My mom and uncles, each of whom went through New York public schools, all eventually earned PhDs and went on to do groundbreaking work in their fields. Each of them had opportunities to choose how their careers unfolded, and they were able to let their interests, curiosity, and passions drive their paths.

I'm sure this story sounds familiar to many children of immigrants. For those whose parents or grandparents grew up with very little, the pressure to succeed economically, in ways that are *visible* within your community, can feel overwhelming- and sometimes stifling. This is certainly true of the eastern-European Jewish community my grandparents came from. I'm so lucky that Aaron, despite this- and even having known starvation and persecution, still wanted to ensure his children's lives were lived with *purpose*, and not just in pursuit of safety and comfort.

## Be honest with yourself about your priorities

When I talk with younger folks about how to steer their careers, and I'm often reminded of Aaron's story. In my field, by the time someone finds themselves in the position of having the choice to go into management, they've already reached a certain level of economic security- which ironically makes it a more complicated choice.

For some, your standing in the eyes of your family or community is an overwhelming factor. No judgement; you don't choose the family or culture you were born into- and I know how important my family's opinion is to me. Hopefully there's some wiggle room in there for you to find a balance that works for you.

If your top priority is wealth, I also get it. Having enough money can sure make life pleasant, though I'm generally skeptical that it helps beyond a certain point. And there's a real risk of that kind of motivation, alone, leaving you hollow.

## Flourishing starts with what you do *every day*

If you've decided, like me, that you want joy to be the primary factor driving your career, my advice is to think about what you want from your career in *concrete, tangible terms*. Think back over the last week: what specific activities you felt excited about, which achievements really mattered to you, what kind of people you enjoyed being around. 

But be honest with yourself. If there's tasks you find you keep putting off, or that once you get through them, you're spent for the day... think hard about taking a new role (even a promotion) that means you'll be doing *more* of that thing.

And of course, any achievement of note requires hard work- and involves frustration and toil. Lots of the things I want to accomplish require that I spend time doing exactly the things that drain me. But by managing my energy deliberately, and checking in with myself, I've been able to build a career that's sustainable, and keeps my fuel tank filled.
